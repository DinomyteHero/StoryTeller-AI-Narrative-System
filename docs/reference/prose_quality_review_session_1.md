## Turn 0

**Scene type:** exploration  |  **Opening passage**

### Narration

*Word count: 612*

You crouch behind the corrugated hull of Jessa Rill's repair bay because leaving the settlement in a hurry would endanger the farms. You had decided, deliberately, that morning that you would not turn every door into a panic; you would choose which people to risk. Now the consequences of that choice sit in the dirt in front of you, loud and bright as an Imperial crest.

The shuttle's ramp sings metal on metal. Two figures in dark armor move down it like a matched pair of knives—slow, economical, watching. They ask about "unusual abilities" the way a farmer asks the price of seed: not curious, already certain. A mechanic three stalls over answers first because he owes favors to too many people and hates the look of uniforms in his doorway. He points out a small house on the edge of the settlement where the curtains always hang drawn. He says nothing about you to spare you; he says everything to save himself. The taller figure inclines its head and makes a notation with a gloved finger; the shorter one kneels and sweeps the air with a compact device that whispers in a frequency that rattles the windowpanes. The device hesitates longer where the medkit sits on your table.

The Force presses against your chest like a closed fist and a name that is not a name—voices from a holocron shard—presses behind it. You know to move, to melt into the fence line and ride the scrub to the eastern dike. You push your breath low and begin to slide, led by that taut certainty.

You don't make the dike.

A child sees you. He is no older than you were when the Temple still had gardens, and he has damp hair from the wells and a fascination with small things. He points, because pointing is easier than hiding. One of the armored figures spins at that simple human motion. The shorter one catches the movement with the small device, and its lens blinks a hungry red. Your concealed saber thrums once in its hollow like a throat clearing—subtle, but not invisible to a machine that listens for the Force.

They do not run to you yet. They close the distance instead, their boots turning the wind, their words clipped and patient. The taller one asks less and watches more; the shorter one speaks in a voice like cold ledger paper, asking the mechanic for the woman's name. A drift of neighbors gathers, trying to be useful, offering grain or a false name, or worse: giving a name they think will satisfy. You feel the settlement searching you as if you are already a map they must read.

You are exposed and small. The holocron shard warms in your pocket, sending you the memory of a breathing exercise you learned once—slow air, center, awareness. You consider stepping forward, speaking, claiming to be a traveling healer. That would be to stand in the light and risk everything you have hidden. You consider slipping into the swampy dike and leaving before anyone notices the absence. Neither feels safe. Both feel like choosing the kind of person you will be if you survive this morning.

The taller figure turns in your direction. The mechanic, flustered, calls out a name he plucks from a memory of a woman who used to barter parts for stitches. The armored man acknowledges the name with a nod that ends nowhere good. The Force pulls at you with a warning and a demand: run, or be honest, or be clever enough to lie without being seen. The settlement holds its breath on a wind that smells faintly of burned wiring.

### Choices Offered

1. Use the holocron breath to steady yourself, step from the shadows, and offer to treat the Injured on the shuttle — you will speak with precise words, modest, useful. If they press, you will have to reveal what you are willing to reveal.
2. Slip into the ditch now, moving through the water and reeds the way you used to move through temple gardens — slow, patient, invisible. The farms will notice a missing neighbor later; you will not be noticed now.
3. Reach out with the Force and scan the officers' intentions to find which one doubts and might be reasoned with. You know the risk: a focused reach might flare on the device they carried.
4. Sit on the hull and let the shard's memory of the breathing exercise settle you, turning the panic into a decision rather than a reflex.

The officer who doubts tightens his grip on something at his belt, but his jaw slackens as if he’s hearing a different timetable of what duty can be.

Outside, the settlement freezes with the simple practicalities of small places — a dog pads under a table, a child’s laugh cuts off like a snapped wire. You are a whisper against corrugated metal and the sudden, brittle attention of strangers. You can step into that attention. You can turn and run down the service lane, the farms beyond still worth saving if you vanish now. Or you can lean into the one loose thread of humanity you felt and see if it will hold you both.

Your breath is quiet. Your voice, if you use it, will be measured and small, because you have learned that a measured word can be stronger than an outstretched blade.

### Narration

*Word count: 397*

You stand, palms flat against corrugated metal, and call him by the name the jittery mechanic fumbled earlier. "I am a healer. I will not hurt your people." The words come small, deliberate; the syllables are tools, not entreaties. Air moves across your montrals. The officer turns.

He is not the taller man's certainty; up close, his uniform fits like a promise he is still trying to keep. His jaw works around the syllables you hand him. He tells you, in a voice that tries for command and lands on tired, "We have orders to check—" and stops, because the mechanic's shout behind him breaks into the sentence like a pebble into water.

You wanted calm to be armor. The attempt splinters. The officer's eyes flick to the mechanic, then back to you; skepticism is carved into the tiny, mechanical motions of his hands. He does not lower his blaster, but his fingers curl less tightly than they did when you first watched them. He says, more to himself than to you, "You shouldn't be here." The syllables are accusation and question both. He searches your face for something—fear, deceit, advantage—and finds steadiness instead. It does not convince him; it complicates him.

A senior of the shuttle paces a step forward, and a shadow moves under the ramp like a hand testing a grip. The taller man clears his throat; metal clicks—the sound of a protocol being consulted. The middle officer exhales, a small, sharp thing, and something in his bearing loosens: an almost-grin at a joke that never landed. The advantage you carved with a single honest line is this — the doubt you read when you reached outward finds purchase, however small, in the man. He will hesitate a beat longer than the others.

He asks a question that is not about you but about the farms: "Who did you treat this week?" The inquiry is practical and thinly veiled as a search for contradiction. In the pause, you hear a girl somewhere past the workshop laughing; the sound is brittle as glass but still whole. The smell of oil and rain-scorched grass knots at the back of your throat. Your medkit, warm in the hollow of your robes, feels like the sum of every life you let yourself touch.

You do not talk faster. You choose the answer that will be true and useful.

### Choices Offered

1. Offer to check the nearest moisture farm for injured people right now — show I am healer by action, not words. This is straightforward; they will see what you do.
2. Probe him gently with the Force for the doubt you felt—feel whether he will bend toward mercy or orders, knowing the reach might flare.
3. Explain how you survived Order 66 in a single, true sentence and then fall silent—trust the weight of truth to change him or to harden him. (Introspective choice)
4. Ask for one civilian as guarantor—a farmer to vouch for you while you treat the settlement—accepting temporary restraint to prove your intent.

### Narration

*Word count: 457*

You reach with the Force the way you trace a pulse — fingertip against the underside of a braided wire — and it answers like a hand pressed back. The probe is small, precise: skepticism folded into habit, the thing you felt earlier made audible now as rhythm and edge. It does not burn or flare; it hums, clean and quiet. For a second the middle officer's doubt becomes a single detail you can hold: a tightness at the jaw, a breath kept too long, an argument rehearsed and then abandoned.

He does not step forward. He meets your name with a thin, military courtesy and the set of a man who has practiced refusing mercy until the muscle is elided. The taller officer shifts his weight so the sun glints off the Imperial crest on his sleeve. The mechanic's hands fidget where you can see them; he is still trying to be useful by being noisy.

Your words — healer, not harm — are true, and your Force reach confirms a sliver of openness. The political work, the convincing, fails anyway. The officer's shoulders do not fold. He keeps his hand near the strap on his belt in a manner meant to be invisible. He asks a question meant to make you contradict yourself: "Whom did you see? Names." Not an accusation, not yet; a tool.

There is a useful thing in the failure. When you let the Force draw back, it leaves a clear image: the middle officer's cadence is brittle because he hides uncertainty from himself. He worries about what obedience will cost him if he chooses mercy. That worry is a seam you could pick at later, if you have time and if you can make kindness look like a plan rather than a risk.

Someone laughs — a child's laugh somewhere beyond the repair bay — and the sound is too sharp. The medkit in your robes is warm against your ribs, familiar as a promise. You taste rain on the air and oil; you taste the memory of a temple lesson about speaking only when you must. You do not hurry. Your hands stay visible, open. Saying less is a choice that keeps a few doors closed and others ajar.

The middle officer's mouth tightens. He does not give you a name. He does not order you taken. He does not let you go. The shuttle's ramp still stands like a gate to another world; the settlement's needs sit where they always have, immediate and real.

You have learned something he does not know he is revealing, and he has learned enough of you to be suspicious without being violent. The space between those facts will be the work you do.

### Choices Offered

1. Step forward, keep your palms open, and answer with the names of the people you treated this week — simple facts shown as trust, not plea. Let him measure you against what he can verify.
2. Walk a slow circle around the shuttle's shadow and speak to the mechanic instead, asking about repairs and the farm's routes; patience and usefulness make you harder to dismiss.
3. Hold the holocron shard against your palm and let its echoes steady you; sit with what you learned from the man's conflict before you say anything else.
4. Show him the medkit — not as a threat but as proof of work done here — and offer to treat one small injury on the spot to prove your purpose. The offer risks exposing you but makes your intentions concrete.

The taller officer watches you the way someone watches a contained fire—respectful distance, calculating how the flame might be banked or sparked. His device, strapped along his forearm, glints with plates and wires you do not know. You know better than to reach into other people's tools without reason. He will obey certainty.
